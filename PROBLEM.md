# The problem

Rare-earth-free permanent magnets are screened by their intrinsic properties: magnetisation, anisotropy, Curie point. What a magnet delivers also depends on alignment, how well the easy axes of its grains line up, which sets its remanence and energy product. For most candidate phases nobody knows whether any practical process can align them. The hard phase often forms from a parent phase by a solid-state transformation, so the alignment of the parent, from casting, directional solidification or additive manufacturing, survives only if the transformation keeps it, and a magnetic field applied during formation acts only if the forming phase is already magnetic at that temperature.

A few cases are known. AlNiCo is made anisotropic by ageing columnar, directionally solidified grains in a field just below the Curie point of the forming Fe-Co-rich phase. In printed MnAl(C), the parent ε phase was strongly textured, but after 5 min at 580 °C the hard τ phase had no strong preferred orientation (Pacheco et al., Alloys 2 (2023) 100, doi:10.3390/alloys2020007). τ-MnAl is ferromagnetic only below about 570 to 630 K, and textured MnAl-C magnets have come from hot extrusion.

## To find

For the rare-earth-free hard magnetic phases under serious study: which processing routes can align them, the most remanence each route could deliver and why, and the single measurement that would test each prediction.

## Given

- The cases above, as the floor and as cases any account must reproduce.
- Today's practice for comparison: sintered NdFeB, aligned by pressing in a magnetic field.

## Why it is not routine

Alignment is decided by a chain of properties that are rarely measured together: the orientation relationship and variant selection of the transformation, the magnetic order of the forming phase at its formation temperature, twinning, and the anisotropy of the result. The evidence is scattered across decades of papers, many available only as abstracts, and many phases have never been aligned at all.
